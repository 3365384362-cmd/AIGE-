Here’s how it works. First, a student answers a question and shows their work. The system records the attempt and whether the student needed a hint.

[按 →，粒子重组为诊断模型]

Next, the system looks for a pattern. One wrong answer could be a small mistake. The same mistake across several questions may show that the student needs help with a specific idea. If the pattern is unclear, the tutor can ask one more question to check.

[按 →，粒子重组为提示和复测]

Finally, the tutor gives a hint and asks a new question. That new answer helps it decide what to do next. If the student gets it right without a hint, they may be ready for a harder problem.

Research notes

Start with a conditional-probability problem. Store the answer, reasoning where needed, skill tag, and hint use. Treat timing and navigation as weak signals since access needs and interruptions affect both.

Reveal one: knowledge tracing estimates mastery from a series of responses and allows for guesses and slips. Contrasting items help distinguish a misconception from a one-off error. Reveal two: choose prerequisite practice, another explanation or a harder problem. After feedback, use fresh unaided evidence to recalibrate the estimate. Route persistent or ambiguous cases to an instructor. Sources [2, 5].

Research notes

ALEKS fits domains with structured skill prerequisites and a suitable item bank. ASU documents real math placement and an individualized practice module. The product and ASU documentation establish use rather than a causal effect on achievement. Sources [10–11].

CS50 Duck provides help between office hours and is aligned with course policy. Dialogue history alone offers weak evidence of mastery. CS50 documentation establishes use rather than a grade or retention effect. Source [12].

Jill Watson supports online and blended learners outside staff hours. The research paper reports classroom deployments at Georgia Tech and two community colleges. Coverage depends on the materials available, and the deployment evaluation does not establish a causal learning gain. Source [13].

Learning gains depend on the tutoring design

Structured tutoring helped. A two-lesson crossover study in 194 Harvard physics students found higher immediate scores.

Access alone showed no clear gain. A semester-long university chatbot trial with about 500 students found no significant achievement or engagement effect.

Test again after help is removed. A high-school experiment found that an unrestricted chatbot lifted practice scores while lowering the subsequent unaided exam score.

A different university study ran for a full semester. It found no clear improvement in grades or engagement from offering a chatbot. The two studies tested different ways of using AI, so we should pay attention to how the tool is used.

[按 →，出现无 AI 考试结果]

And in a high-school math study, students with a chatbot that could give them answers did better in practice. On a test without AI, they scored seventeen percent lower than the group that practiced without the chatbot. That’s why we need to check what students can do after the help is gone.

Research notes

Kestin et al. [5] compared a self-paced GPT-4 instructional package with classroom active learning, randomized in a two-lesson crossover. Immediate scores improved. Students reported greater engagement and motivation. Median tutor time was 49 minutes versus approximately 60 minutes of classroom instruction, with different timing measures. The study does not establish long-term retention or isolate which component helped.

Reveal one: Thoeni and Fryer [7] randomized access to a course-grounded chatbot for about 500 undergraduates over a semester and found no significant achievement, engagement, or interest effects. Bowen et al. [4] randomized 605 students at six public campuses and reported similar outcomes for machine-guided hybrid statistics and conventional formats. Ma et al. [3] pooled 107 effects from 14,321 learners, estimating a positive average for older intelligent tutoring systems (random-effects g = 0.41), with results varying by comparator.

Reveal two: Bastani et al. [6] studied nearly 1,000 high-school math students. The unrestricted GPT condition improved assisted practice by 48% relative to control and scored 17% lower on the unaided exam. The guarded tutor mostly avoided that penalty. This is adjacent evidence for universities. Two preprints report favorable unaided outcomes. Contractor and Reyes [8] observed a smaller gain at one week. Li et al. [9] report better proctored exam performance in a 19-university rollout of instructor-designed tools. Both remain preliminary.

During the term, the tutor can suggest practice. Teachers can look at repeated mistakes and step in when needed. If many students make the same mistake, the teacher can spend a few minutes on it in the next class.

Research notes

Design: audit accuracy and accessibility, restrict data retention and vendor reuse, and give students a way to correct records or choose a non-AI route. Use vetted knowledge sources and checks for generated explanations.

Reveal one: use a brief baseline diagnostic and add adaptive exercises in the learning management system. Have staff review flags, verify the evidence, correct misconceptions and update the item bank. Avoid assigning grades from an uncertain mastery estimate. Ask for attempts and fade support to limit overreliance.

Reveal two: randomize comparable sections when feasible and account for clustering and attrition. Use a delayed novel task as the primary learning outcome. Report retention, knowledge, time-to-mastery, engagement and motivation separately. Audit differential error rates, learning and access across groups. Review workload, cost and any safety incidents when deciding whether to expand. Sources [5, 7, 9, 14].
